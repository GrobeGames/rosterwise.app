> **Status as of October 8, 2026.** The NCAA Division I Cabinet affirmed this proposal on October 7, 2026, but not with the 75% of the vote needed to make it final right away. It is now in a 30-day rescission period. It takes effect for the 2027-28 academic year unless "two-thirds of DI members (at least 245 schools and/or conferences)" vote to rescind it. The NCAA has not published the vote count or the date the window closes. We will update this post when the outcome is known.

You may have seen headlines that college soccer is becoming a fall-and-spring sport. Two details matter for your family before anything else.

First, this applies to **Division I men's soccer only**. Women's soccer is not changing, and the proposal is Division I legislation, so it does not change Division II, Division III, NAIA, or NJCAA seasons.

Second, it is **not final yet**. Below is what the NCAA actually decided, what the season would look like, who it would affect, and what is still undecided.

## What the NCAA decided, step by step

The rule is NCAA Division I Proposal No. 2026-27, introduced by the Division I Men's Soccer Oversight Committee in January 2026. It has been through three steps since.

**May 13, 2026 — adopted by the committee.** The Men's Soccer Oversight Committee adopted the two-semester season, effective August 1, 2027, subject to review by the Division I Cabinet.

**June 24, 2026 — sent back.** The Cabinet remanded the proposal, meaning it returned it to the committee for more work. The NCAA reported that 20 Division I conferences opposed the change, raising concerns about the length of the season, the lack of clarity about related rules, and the impact on championship selections. The Cabinet also raised concerns about the student-athlete academic experience, and asked for more information about midyear enrollees and scheduling challenges at individual schools.

**October 7, 2026 — affirmed, narrowly.** After the committee answered those questions, the Cabinet affirmed the proposal. The NCAA's own headline describes the vote as narrow. Because it fell short of 75%, the proposal entered the 30-day rescission period described in the status note above.

## What the season would look like

If the rule survives, it changes **when** men's soccer is played, not **how much**.

**The totals stay the same.** The 132-day playing season and the 25-contest annual maximum (which includes scrimmages and exhibitions) are unchanged.

**Fall segment.** Up to 18 contests — 15 regular-season games plus 3 scrimmages or exhibitions — from late August to the week before Thanksgiving. Each team must play at least 8 regular-season games in the fall.

**Spring segment.** Up to 10 contests — 8 regular-season games plus 2 scrimmages or exhibitions — starting in mid-February. Each team must play at least 3 regular-season games in the spring. Scrimmages and exhibitions do not count toward either minimum.

Because 18 plus 10 is more than the 25-contest annual cap, no team can play the maximum in both segments. Each program decides how to split its schedule.

**The College Cup moves to spring.** Today the season runs in a 10- to 13-week fall window, and the Men's College Cup is in December. Under the new model, the national championship would be played in the spring. The NCAA has **not set the dates**; it says the committee will work with NCAA staff and broadcast partners on timing during the rescission period.

**Conference championships.** Each conference would decide whether its own championship is played in the fall or the spring segment.

**One transfer window instead of two.** In its May announcement, the NCAA said that starting in 2027-28, men's soccer would have one 15-consecutive-day transfer window in the spring, opening the day after the national championship. That replaces today's 45 days split across two windows. Because the championship dates are not set, neither is the window.

**Midyear players.** Two eligibility answers in the NCAA's question-and-answer document for the proposal matter for families:

- A freshman who enrolls full-time in January, attends the first day of classes, and is otherwise eligible **can play** in the spring segment.
- A player who competed for one Division I school in the fall and transfers at midyear **cannot play** for a new Division I school that winter or spring.

**Rest built in.** Teams would get two additional consecutive days off in the fall segment and three in the spring segment, plus an optional 7-day transition period before each segment. Neither counts toward the 132 days.

## What is not changing

**Women's soccer.** The NCAA's question-and-answer document says the proposal "is specific to men's soccer and does not impact the women's soccer playing and practice season legislation." The Division I Women's Soccer Oversight Committee reviewed the proposal and declined to propose a similar change.

**This season.** Nothing in the proposal takes effect before August 1, 2027. The 2026 men's season is played in the current format, ending with the College Cup in December.

**This year's recruiting calendar.** The published 2026-27 Division I recruiting calendar still applies, including the men's soccer dead period on December 11–14, 2026, which is tied to the championship weekend. The NCAA's materials on this proposal do not address recruiting periods, contact dates, or visits.

## Who it would affect

This is our reading of the August 1, 2027 effective date, not an NCAA statement, and it assumes the rule survives rescission.

- **High school class of 2027 and later (men's):** the first recruits whose entire college careers would be played under the two-semester model.
- **Current college players:** their seasons from 2027-28 on would be played under the new model.
- **Women's soccer recruits of every class:** no change from this decision.

## Where the idea came from

This is not the first attempt. In 2019 the Atlantic Coast Conference, Big Ten Conference, and Pac-12 Conference sponsored Division I Proposal 2019-90, an "academic year" model with 14 contests in the fall and 9 in the spring. The NCAA's legislative database shows it was tabled in April 2020 because of the COVID-19 pandemic, tabled again in April 2022 pending the work of the NCAA's Transformation Committee, and recorded as defeated in May 2023 "based on application of the sunset provision." It never reached a final vote.

The current proposal came after a white paper from U.S. Soccer's NextGen College Soccer Committee in October 2025. The NCAA describes Proposal 2026-27 as "a distinct and independent proposal." Unlike the white paper, it does not lengthen the season or create a regionalized schedule with tiers or promotion and relegation.

## The case for it, and the concerns

In its May announcement, the NCAA listed the benefits it sees for players: a more balanced academic and athletic load, with lower time demands in any single term; potentially fewer midweek games and less missed class; more predictable weekly schedules; more recovery time between matches; and more time for campus life. In October, the NCAA added that advocates, "including student-athlete representatives who serve on the Cabinet," point to more rest between games and less weekday travel.

The concerns on record are the ones the 20 opposing conferences and the Cabinet raised in June: the length of the season, clarity about related rules, championship selection, and the academic experience. The NCAA has not named the 20 conferences.

## What is still undecided

- **Whether it survives rescission.** The NCAA has not published the vote count or the closing date of the 30-day window.
- **When the spring College Cup will be played**, and therefore when the single transfer window would open.
- **The recruiting calendar for the new season.** Today's men's soccer dead period is anchored to a December championship. The NCAA's materials on the proposal do not say how that would work once the championship moves.
- **Roster limits across two semesters.** At schools in the House settlement, Division I men's soccer rosters are limited to 28 players. The proposal materials do not address how roster-limit rules would apply when the season spans two terms.

We will not guess at any of these. When the NCAA publishes answers, we will update this post.

## Questions to ask coaches

If your athlete is a men's soccer recruit in the class of 2027 or later, these are fair questions for any Division I staff:

1. **"If the two-semester season takes effect, how do you expect to split your games between fall and spring?"** Programs will have room to choose, within the minimums and the 25-game cap.
2. **"What would the spring semester look like academically — travel, missed class, finals?"** The academic experience was one of the Cabinet's concerns, so it is worth hearing each program's answer.
3. **"Do you bring in January enrollees, and does a spring season change how you use them?"** Midyear freshmen would be eligible for the spring segment.
4. **"How would a single spring transfer window affect how you build your roster?"** The answer tells you something about how much a staff relies on transfers.
5. **"What does preseason look like for an incoming freshman in August 2027?"**

## What this means for your family right now

For 2026-27, nothing changes: the season, the College Cup, and the recruiting calendar all run as published. For men's recruits in the class of 2027 and later, the two-semester season is worth understanding now, because it would shape the calendar of every college season they play if it takes effect. For women's soccer families, this decision does not change your athlete's season.
