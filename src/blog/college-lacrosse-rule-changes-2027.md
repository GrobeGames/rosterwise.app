> **Status as of October 8, 2026.** Everything below comes from the NCAA's own committee reports, announcements, and championship pages. The NCAA described the changes in reports and news releases; the exact rule wording will appear in its updated rules materials. Where the NCAA has not said something, we say so rather than fill the gap.

This summer the NCAA approved 13 rule changes for women's college lacrosse and five for men's, and its committees confirmed the championship arrangements for May 2027. Some of these changes affect how a game is played and officiated. Others are only being tested this fall and are not rules.

If your family is following college lacrosse because your athlete is being recruited, here is what changed, what it means on the field, and what to ask coaches.

## The short version

**Women's lacrosse (13 changes, effective for the 2026-27 academic year):**

- Every yellow card is now a two-minute penalty served in full.
- Targeting is a new foul that draws a red card.
- A red card still means ejection from the game, but no longer an automatic suspension for the next game.
- Flopping and embellishment are combined into one foul.
- The end-of-quarter possession rule goes back to the 2025 power-play rule.
- Only coaches may request video review.
- Officials check the draw players' sticks every quarter.
- Equipment that needs certification must be on sale by January 1 before the season.

**Men's lacrosse (five changes, approved for all three NCAA divisions):**

- A fluorescent green or yellow ball is allowed in 2027 and required in 2028.
- Overtime is played in 15-minute periods until someone scores.
- Coaches can challenge a goal for offsides, too many players, or a ball carrier stepping on a boundary line.
- Officials can use video to review possible head and neck contact they did not call, and to take back a head or neck penalty.

**Championships:** Divisions I, II, and III women's lacrosse hold a joint championship at Towson University in May 2027. The men's championships return to Philadelphia, at Lincoln Financial Field.

## Women's lacrosse: 13 rule changes

**How they were approved.** The NCAA Division I Women's Lacrosse Rules Subcommittee and the Divisions II and III Women's Lacrosse Rules Committee met jointly June 15–18, 2026, and approved 13 rules-change proposals "for the 2027 season." The Division I Women's Lacrosse Oversight Committee adopted all 13 at its July 28–29, 2026 annual meeting, and the Divisions II and III Playing Rules Oversight Panel also approved them. The NCAA announced the changes on August 12, 2026, "effective for the 2026-27 academic year," which is the season played in spring 2027.

Here are the ones that matter most to players and parents.

### Every yellow card is a two-minute penalty, served in full

All yellow cards become two-minute, nonreleasable penalties. Under the current rules book, a player serving a releasable yellow card can return when the other team scores. A nonreleasable penalty is different: the player serves the full two minutes, and her team plays a player down for that whole time, even if the other team scores.

The rules committees' stated reason is to standardize yellow-card penalties and make them simpler to administer.

### Targeting is a new red-card foul

Targeting is added as a new flagrant foul, and a player who commits it gets a red card. The NCAA's definition is "any action in which a player initiates forcible contact with an opponent without making a legitimate attempt to play the ball and where the primary purpose of the contact is to strike, hit or otherwise make significant physical contact with a high potential for injury rather than to contest possession."

The committees said current rules did not specifically address dangerous contact delivered without a legitimate attempt to play the ball, particularly away from the ball.

### A red card no longer means missing the next game

A player who receives a red card is still ejected from that game. What changes is that the automatic suspension for the next game has been eliminated. The committees' reasoning was that officials may be more willing to issue a red card when it is warranted if it does not automatically sideline a player for the following game.

### Flopping and embellishment become one foul

The two fouls are combined into a single foul, flopping: exaggerating, dramatizing, or simulating contact to get an official to call or escalate a foul or card. It is classified as a flagrant foul, with a yellow card and a two-minute nonreleasable penalty. Because it now carries a yellow card, a possible flop also becomes eligible for video review.

### The end-of-quarter possession rule goes back to the 2025 version

For 2026, any team in possession at the end of the first and third quarters kept possession to start the next quarter, regardless of whether it had a player advantage. That rule "is no longer applicable." Instead, the 2025 power-play rule returns, with one modification: if a team with a numerical advantage due to penalties has possession when a quarter or overtime period ends, it gets possession to begin the next one.

### Only coaches may request video review

Only coaches may now request a video review. Officials must tell both teams' coaches whenever a review has been requested, and they should try to finish a review within two minutes. The NCAA also announced related video-review changes:

- Officials can use video to identify or confirm the correct offender whenever a yellow or red card is given, not only for cards that result in an ejection.
- After a goal, a review request must come before the draw clock runs out. During a timeout, it must come within the first 30 seconds.

### Officials check the draw players' sticks every quarter

Officials must check both draw players' sticks at least once each quarter, immediately before a draw, and again before the opening draw of each overtime period. The check covers pocket depth and ball movement on the front and back of each stick. The committees said the change is meant to discourage gamesmanship involving draw sticks.

### Equipment that needs certification must be on sale by January 1

Any equipment that needs certification or governing-body approval must be commercially available to all schools by January 1 before the spring season. Equipment that does not meet that test cannot be used that season.

### The other changes

- Players may exchange sticks with a teammate on the field when they do not have the ball.
- The game clock and possession clock stop during official warnings and during defensive false starts on an 8-meter free position.
- Stick stringing rules are clarified, and some stringing becomes illegal. Mesh pockets are allowed if the gap between pocket and head is no wider than two nylons (0.6 cm).
- Stick and body contact fouls are reorganized into two categories, illegal check and illegal contact, and the egregious-conduct and misconduct fouls are revised.

## Men's lacrosse: five rule changes

**How they were approved.** The NCAA Division I Men's Lacrosse Oversight Committee approved five changes from its rules subcommittee at its July 27–28, 2026 annual meeting. The NCAA announced on July 28, 2026, that the changes were approved for all three divisions, with the Divisions II and III Playing Rules Oversight Panel approving them the week before.

### A new ball color

The standard ball becomes fluorescent green or yellow, "similar to hex code #ccff00." The committee's report says it is allowed for the 2027 season and fully required by the 2028 season. The NCAA's announcement puts it the same way in academic years: recommended for 2026-27 and required for 2027-28. The stated reason is visibility for players, officials, spectators, and television audiences.

### Overtime: 15-minute periods

Overtime will be played in 15-minute periods until a goal is scored. Previously, it was played in four-minute periods, with teams switching sides of the field, until the winning goal. Timeout and challenge rules do not change.

The NCAA's announcement and the committee report do not say which season the overtime change takes effect.

### Coaches can challenge more goals

A coach may challenge a goal that could be wiped out for any of these:

- Offsides.
- Too many players on the field.
- The ball carrier stepping on a boundary line. The report defines boundary lines as the lines around the field plus the midline and the restraining lines.

The challenge is allowed only if the goal came within 15 seconds of the violation and no whistle sounded in between. If the game clock is not shown on the video, officials may use a stopwatch while reviewing the play at actual speed.

### Head and neck contact on video

Officials may start a video review of possible contact to the head or neck even if they did not call a penalty. If they did call a head or neck penalty, they may take it back if video shows there was no illegal contact.

## This fall's experiments are not rules

Both sports are testing ideas in fall play only. The NCAA lists them as experimental rules for fall competition, separate from the adopted changes above, and we are not predicting whether any of them will become rules.

**Women's lacrosse experiments.** During the fall, teams can try:

- A 60-second possession clock, instead of 90 seconds, after a team gains possession from a draw, or is awarded possession instead of a draw.
- Ten players on the field instead of 12, with six players instead of seven required below the restraining line.
- Awarding possession to the other team at the spot of the ball if an attacking team fails to keep possession below the restraining line, except for a deflected shot.

**Men's lacrosse experiment.** In fall competition in all three divisions, teams can try a rule that a ball released from below the restraining line on a team's own defensive end counts as a shot only if it scores or hits the goal piping. The NCAA said it is aimed at teams facing a time violation that throw the ball from their defensive end without a real shot attempt, and the committee wants feedback from teams that use it.

## Championships in May 2027

### Women's: one weekend for all three divisions

The Division II and Division III women's lacrosse committees recommended accepting Division I's invitation to create a joint DI/DII/DIII women's lacrosse championship for 2027 and 2028, and the NCAA's championship pages now list all three divisions' 2027 finals at Towson.

- **Division I:** May 28–30, 2027, at Johnny Unitas Stadium, Towson University, in Towson, Maryland.
- **Division II and Division III championship games:** both on May 29, 2027, at Towson.

The Division III committee's report describes the weekend this way: DII and DIII play their title games on Saturday, between the DI semifinals on Friday and the DI final on Sunday. The joint championship moves to Subaru Park in Philadelphia in 2028.

The earlier rounds change too:

- **Division II:** first round May 13, second round May 15, quarterfinals May 20, and semifinals May 22, 2027, with the semifinals at campus sites. The final is May 29.
- **Division III:** the first round moves to the Wednesday after selections. In 2027, the semifinals are May 16 and the final is May 29, with a weekend off in between. The committee noted that teams playing in the first round could play three games in four days if they advance to the third round.

### Men's: back to Philadelphia

The Division I men's championship is May 29–31, 2027, at Lincoln Financial Field in Philadelphia, hosted by Drexel University. Brown University and the U.S. Naval Academy host the quarterfinals May 22–23, 2027. The NCAA's Division II and Division III men's championship pages list their 2027 title games on May 30, 2027, also at Lincoln Financial Field.

## What it means for a recruit

These are playing-rule and championship changes, not recruiting legislation. They change how college games are played and officiated, and a few are worth understanding if your athlete is heading into the college game.

**Discipline costs more time in women's lacrosse.** Under the 2027 rules, every yellow card leaves a team a player down for the full two minutes. Under the current rules book, a team could get a carded player back when the other team scored.

**The draw stick gets checked every quarter.** A draw specialist's stick will be checked for pocket depth and ball movement at least once a quarter. A stick that meets the pocket and stringing rules matters for any player who takes draws.

**Equipment timing.** Gear that needs certification has to be on sale by January 1 to be used that spring. If your athlete will be a college freshman, it is worth asking the program what equipment it provides and what players buy themselves.

**Championship weekend and recruiting.** On the 2026-27 Division I women's lacrosse recruiting calendar, the championship weekend (May 28–30, 2027) is a dead period with one exception. Coaches may evaluate at one event held that weekend within 100 miles of the championship site, but may not attend it from two hours before through two hours after the semifinals, or from two hours before through two hours after the final.

**The experiments are only experiments.** If your athlete plays in a fall college game or watches one using 10-a-side play or a shorter possession clock, that is a test, not the 2027 rule.

## Questions to ask coaches

1. **"How do you coach discipline now that every women's yellow card is a full two minutes?"** The answer tells you how a staff thinks about aggressive defense under the new penalty rules.
2. **"Who takes your draws, and how do you handle the stick check every quarter?"** This matters most for a recruit who wants to take draws in college.
3. **"What equipment does the program provide, and what do players buy themselves?"** It helps to know this before your athlete buys anything new.
4. **"Did your team use any of the fall experimental rules, and what did you think?"** Coaches can tell you how the tests played out in their practices and games, without anyone predicting what the NCAA will adopt.
5. **"For men's programs: how does your staff use coach's challenges and video review?"** The expanded challenge is new for 2027, and each staff will have its own approach.
