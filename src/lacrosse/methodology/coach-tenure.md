## Why coaching tenure matters

The head coach leads a college lacrosse program's recruiting and its approach on the field, and shapes the day-to-day experience of being on the team. A recruit chooses a school, but plays for a coach — so it is worth knowing how long the current coach has been there.

Coaching tenure — how long the head coach has been at the program — is a simple public data point. Here is what it can and can't tell you.

**Long tenure means continuity so far.** A coach who has been at a program for a decade has had time to recruit the classes on the current roster. That tells you about the past; it does not tell you how long the coach will stay.

**Short tenure means a transition.** A coach in their first or second year is working with players the previous staff recruited, alongside their own first classes.

**No tenure data exists in isolation.** A long-tenured coach might be changing the program; a new coach might keep much of what was there. Tenure is context, not conclusion.

## What long tenure can mean

Where a coach has been at a program for many years, these are the things worth looking at:

**Recruiting patterns you can see.** Several recruiting classes from one staff sit on the same roster, so the roster's previous schools, clubs, and hometowns show where that staff has recruited. The roster shows the pattern so far; it can't promise next year's class will follow it.

**A playing style you can watch.** A coach who has been in place for years has seasons of games that show how the team plays. Ask the coach to describe it, then watch for yourself, and judge whether it suits your athlete.

**Players who know the program.** Current and former players under the same coach can tell you what daily life on the team is like.

**A roster that reflects one staff's choices.** When one staff has recruited most of the roster, its positions, sizes, and pathways reflect that staff's decisions. A first-year coach's roster mostly reflects the previous staff's.

**What long tenure doesn't tell you:** whether the program is improving, whether its approach suits your athlete, or how long the coach plans to stay. Tenure measures time, not quality.

## What short tenure can mean

Coaches in their first one to three years present a different picture:

**Roster transition.** A new coach inherits players recruited by the previous staff and begins recruiting their own classes; some inherited players may stay and some may leave. If the athletics website still shows last season's roster, compare it with the current one to see what has changed, and ask the coach about their plans.

**Less to go on.** With a new coach there is less track record at this program — fewer seasons and fewer former players — to tell you what it will be like under them. Ask about their previous stops and what they intend to keep or change.

**New connections.** A new coach may recruit from different clubs, schools, or regions than the previous staff did, which can open a door for a player the previous staff wasn't recruiting.

**More open questions.** Joining a program during a coaching transition means more is unsettled. Weigh that against what your family values.

**Something to ask about:** a program that has had several head coaches in a short period. The reasons vary, and the roster won't tell you what they are — ask the current coach or the athletic department what has changed and what the program's plans are.

## What a coaching transition means for current recruits

This is the scenario that families fear most: your athlete commits to a program, and the head coach leaves.

**Verbal commitments are not binding for either party.** A verbal commitment to a program is essentially a commitment to the coaching staff. When the coach leaves, the moral and practical foundation of that commitment changes. Players are free to reopen their recruiting process.

**Written Offers of Athletics Aid are binding to the institution.** With the elimination of the National Letter of Intent in Division I in October 2024, the written offer of athletics aid is the primary financial commitment document there (Division II schools may still use the NLI). If a player has signed a Written Offer of Athletics Aid, the financial commitment from the institution remains in effect even if the coach leaves. However, the playing-time expectations, positional plans, and cultural promises that accompanied the offer may not survive the coaching change.

**New coaches evaluate the existing roster.** A new coach arrives with their own plans and their own recruiting targets. Players recruited by the previous coach may or may not fit them. At a Division I school covered by the House settlement, roster limits of 48 for men's lacrosse and 38 for women's (Bylaw 17.2) also cap how many players the program can carry.

**The honest advice:** If your athlete has a verbal commitment and the coach leaves, have a direct conversation with the incoming coach. Ask specifically about their plans for your athlete's position, their recruiting philosophy, and whether they see your athlete in their vision for the program. Don't assume continuity.

## How to factor tenure into your evaluation

**If stability matters most to your family:** look at how long the head coach has been in place, ask current players whether the team's culture has been consistent during their time, and ask whether the head coach and key assistants have worked together for multiple years.

**If your family is open to a program in transition:** a new coach at a program that fits your athlete academically and geographically may be recruiting for different needs than the previous staff did. Ask what they are looking for at your athlete's position.

**For Division I programs specifically:** at schools covered by the House settlement, roster limits and each school's own scholarship decisions now sit alongside any coaching change. Ask a new coach how they are handling both at your athlete's position.

**For all families:** Know the tenure before you visit. A 30-minute conversation with a coach who has been at the program for 15 years is a different experience than a 30-minute conversation with a coach in their first season. Both can be informative, but the context for evaluating what they tell you is different.

**Pair tenure with roster analysis.** A long-tenured coach's roster tells you what they've built over time. A new coach's roster tells you what they inherited and, in their second or third year, what they're starting to change. The roster is the coach's resume written in player selections.

## The bottom line

Head coach tenure is one data point among many. It doesn't tell you everything about a program, and it shouldn't be the sole basis for any recruiting decision. But it is useful context for understanding where a program has been, and for knowing which questions to ask about where it is going.

Stability isn't everything. Change isn't everything. But knowing which one you're walking into — that's information every family deserves.
