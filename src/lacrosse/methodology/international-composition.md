## Why international composition matters in lacrosse

Every roster spot occupied by an international player is a spot not available to a domestic recruit, and vice versa. That isn't a value judgment about anyone; it's arithmetic. For a family trying to assess where their athlete fits, the international composition of a program's roster is useful practical information.

This matters more after the [House v. NCAA settlement](/lacrosse/guide/house-settlement-and-lacrosse/), which set Division I roster limits of 48 (men's) and 38 (women's) at the schools it covers (2026-27 NCAA Division I Manual, Bylaw 17.2). With a fixed limit, every international roster spot is one less spot for a domestic player, and every domestic spot is one less for an international recruit.

Say one men's program's roster is a quarter Canadian and another's has almost no Canadian players. A domestic recruit faces a different competitive picture at each. Neither is inherently better, but the context matters.

For international recruits — including Canadians considering NCAA lacrosse — the question runs the other way. A roster that already includes players from your country shows that the program has recruited and enrolled international students before. Ask any program how it supports them: visa and I-20 paperwork, academic advising, and, for a player whose background is mainly box lacrosse, the move to the field game.

We had no source for those statements, so we removed them.

## Canada and college lacrosse

**Lacrosse in Canada.** Lacrosse is Canada's national **summer** sport, declared as such by the *National Sports of Canada Act* (1994), which recognises ice hockey as the national winter sport and "the game commonly known as lacrosse" as the national summer sport. The Act does not distinguish box from field lacrosse — a box-specific version of this claim is not in the legislation.

**Box and field.** A Canadian player may have box lacrosse experience as well as field lacrosse. Ask coaches how they evaluate box experience, and how they help a player whose background is mainly box adjust to the field game.

**What is published, and what isn't.** The NCAA does not publish a count of Canadian or international players on college lacrosse rosters; its research on international student-athletes covers first-year players only. Our [International Recruiting in Men's College Lacrosse guide](/lacrosse/mens/guide/international-recruiting/) sets out what the NCAA's own research does report.

## What international composition tells you about a program

Beyond the simple "how many spots are available for domestic recruits" question, a roster's international players can point you to a few useful questions:

**Where the staff has recruited.** A program with players from Canada or elsewhere has recruited there before. Ask whether it still does.

**Experience with international students.** A program that has enrolled international players has been through the paperwork before. Ask how it supports international students now.

**Which positions.** Look at which positions a program's international players hold, not just how many there are.

We had no source for those statements, so we removed them.

## How to use this information

### For domestic recruits

**Factor international composition into your opportunity assessment.** If a program's roster has several international players at your athlete's position, your athlete's competition for a spot there includes them. That isn't a reason to rule the program out — it is part of the full competitive picture.

**Look at where international players are concentrated.** If a program's international players are all at one or two positions, a domestic player at a different position faces less international competition at that program.

**Don't assume programs with high international percentages aren't interested in domestic recruits.** A program with a high international share can still be recruiting domestic players. Ask.

### For Canadian and international recruits

**Look for programs that have enrolled international players before.** A roster with players from outside the U.S. shows the program has done it. Ask any program, with or without that history, what support it offers — I-20/visa processing, academic advising for international students, box-to-field coaching, and help adjusting to campus.

**Look at which provinces (or countries) are represented.** If a program has multiple players from your province or region, there may be a direct connection between the coaching staff and player networks in your area. This can facilitate the recruiting process.

**Ask about the international experience specifically.** How do Canadian/international players integrate into team culture? Is there support for box-to-field transition? Is there support for players adjusting to the American academic system? What is the process for arriving on campus and getting set up?

**Understand the academic side.** Ask how the school's admissions office and the NCAA Eligibility Center will evaluate your athlete's Canadian (or other international) high school record. See our [International Recruiting in Men's College Lacrosse guide](/lacrosse/mens/guide/international-recruiting/) for more detail.

## The data challenge

Determining whether a player is international from roster data isn't always straightforward. Roster pages vary in how they present this information:

- Some list a country of origin for each player (e.g., "Hometown: Toronto, ON, Canada")
- Some list only a hometown and state — or a hometown and province for Canadian players
- Some list only the player's previous school, which might be a Canadian institution or an American school

RosterWise uses the hometown information available on each program's roster to identify international players. When a player's hometown is listed with a non-U.S. country or province, we classify them as international. When the data is ambiguous (for example, a player listed with a U.S. city who may have Canadian origins), we err on the side of not classifying them as international.

This means our international composition numbers are generally conservative — the actual international percentage at some programs may be slightly higher than we report. We believe understating is better than overstating when families are making decisions based on this data.

## Patterns worth watching

**Change over time.** If a program's recent classes include more or fewer international players than its older classes, ask the coach whether that reflects a change in where they recruit.

**Position-specific composition.** The international share at your athlete's position tells you more than the share across the whole team.

**Roster limits.** At Division I schools covered by the House settlement, the roster limit is fixed, so international and domestic players are drawing on the same 48 or 38 spots. How programs are responding is not something we have data on — ask.

## The bigger picture

For recruiting families, the practical question isn't whether international recruiting is good or bad — it's what it means for your athlete's realistic opportunity at a specific program. Understanding international composition is part of the broader roster analysis that turns a vague sense of "maybe that program" into an informed assessment of fit.

The rosters are public. Read them position by position, and take what you find to the coaches.
