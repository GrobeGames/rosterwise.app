## Why the video matters

A recruiting video does a specific job: it lets a coach see your son play without being at the game. Coaches narrow a larger list of athletes down to the ones they recruit seriously, and video is one of the tools they can use to do it. We don't have data on how large those lists are or how much weight any staff puts on video, so ask the coaches you contact.

This matters because:

- Coaches don't have unlimited time to attend every recruiting event
- Coaches don't have unlimited budgets for travel
- Coaches need efficient ways to identify which recruits to invest serious time in
- Video can be the first impression a coach has of your son

For men's lacrosse families, this means the recruiting video isn't a checkbox to complete — it is one of the ways coaches can decide whether to see your son in person.

## How long should the video be?

There is no NCAA rule on video length, and we have no published consensus to point you to. We are not going to invent one.

The practical approach: **ask the coaches you're contacting what length they prefer**, and cut filler clips — any clip that doesn't show a strength costs a coach time. A coach who wants more can ask for more.

**Put your best plays first.** If a coach stops watching early, the part they saw should be your strongest material.

## What the footage can show — by position

Men's lacrosse includes specialized roles such as the long-stick midfielder (LSM) and faceoff specialist (FOGO). Below is what each position's job involves, and so what footage can show. How much weight a given staff puts on each item is a question for that staff — see [What Men's Lacrosse College Coaches Look For By Position](/lacrosse/mens/guide/coaches-look-for-by-position/) for the questions to ask.

### Attack

Footage that shows an attacker's job:

- **Scoring ability** in competitive game situations
- **Two-handed stick skills** (see "What to prioritise in the video" below)
- **Vision and feeding ability** — making the play that creates the goal, not just scoring it
- **Dodging and change of direction** that gets past defenders
- **Confidence with the ball in pressure situations** — leading the offense
- **Communication and offensive organization** — even visible from highlight clips
- **Ride and pressure** — defensive contribution from an attacker

### Midfield

Footage that shows a midfielder's job:

- **Two-way play** — both offensive and defensive contributions
- **Transition play** — running the field, finishing breaks, defending in transition
- **Ground ball play** — winning 50/50 balls
- **Stamina and athleticism** — covering both ends of the field
- **Scoring versatility** — outside shooting, dodging from up top, inside finishing
- **Communication and field awareness**

### Defense

Footage that shows a defender's job:

- **One-versus-one defensive technique** — body position, footwork, stick discipline
- **Stick skills with the long pole** — clearing the ball, making outlet passes
- **Ground ball play in transition** — converting turnovers into offense
- **Caused turnovers** — the defensive equivalent of goals
- **Communication on team defense** — calling slides and switches
- **Slide and recovery work** — how the defender works within team defense

### Long-Stick Midfielder (LSM)

Footage that shows an LSM's job (a midfielder who carries a long pole):

- **Defensive presence at the midfield** — taking the ball from offensive midfielders
- **Ground ball play in transition** — turning ground balls into transition
- **Stick work with the long pole** in space
- **Athleticism** — running and covering in the open field
- **Defensive midfield endurance** — running the length of the field repeatedly

### Face-Off, Get Off (FOGO)

Footage that shows a FOGO's job (a faceoff specialist):

- **Face-off win percentage** in competitive situations
- **Face-off technique** — counter moves, clamp work, exits
- **Ground ball play after the face-off scrum**
- **Athletic ability when the face-off doesn't go as planned**
- **Specific face-off rule compliance** (the position has unique technical rules)

For FOGOs, ask coaches whether they want dedicated faceoff footage. Some FOGOs include faceoff-only segments with close-up footage of clamp work and exits.

### Goalie

Footage that shows a goalie's job:

- **Big saves** — and not just the highlight-reel save, but the consistent save technique
- **Clear-game ability** — outlet passes that create fast-break offense
- **Athletic positioning** — both in the cage and outside it
- **Communication and leadership** — directing the defense, if the footage captures it
- **Body and foot positioning** that demonstrates fundamentals
- **Save consistency across shot types** — high, low, off-stick side, inside, outside

## What to prioritise in the video

Two points are worth thinking about whatever your son's position:

**Two-handed play.** In men's lacrosse, a player who can receive, carry, dodge, shoot and feed with either hand is harder to defend and easier to fit into a system. If your son plays with both hands, make sure the video shows it — deliberately, not incidentally.

**Currency of footage.** Recent footage shows how your son plays now. Refresh the video as new high school, club and tournament footage becomes available.

Ask coaches what they want to see beyond finishing plays — ground balls, riding, effort off the ball, communication. A highlight reel that shows only finishing plays leaves those out.

## Game footage vs. skills footage

**Game footage and skills footage show different things**, and you should ask each coach which they want.

What game footage shows:

- Game footage shows how your athlete performs under pressure against real opponents
- Game footage demonstrates lacrosse IQ — the decisions made in the flow of competition
- Game footage shows how the athlete handles adversity, mistakes, and competitive situations
- Skills footage shows what an athlete can do in a controlled environment, but coaches need to see what he does in real competition

Where skills footage can help:

- **Goalies** may include some controlled-environment save technique footage
- **FOGOs** benefit from dedicated face-off close-up footage that's hard to capture during games
- **New or transferred positions** may need skills footage to demonstrate fundamentals

We don't publish a game-to-skills ratio; ask the coaches you're contacting whether they want skills footage at all.

## What to include alongside the clips

A complete recruiting video should include more than just the highlight clips:

- **Your name and class year** clearly displayed at the start and end
- **Contact information** — your email address and phone number, plus your high school coach's and club coach's contact information
- **Your position(s)** clearly identified
- **Jersey number** identified in each clip so coaches can find you immediately
- **Height, weight, and key athletic measurables** if appropriate
- **High school and club team affiliations**
- **An identifier in each clip** (an arrow, a circle, or a brief highlight) that shows the coach exactly who to watch — especially important for defenders, midfielders, and LSMs where finding the recruit in action can be difficult

Add an identifier to each clip for every field player, including FOGOs. A goalie in the cage may be identifiable without one.

## What to avoid

Several common mistakes weaken otherwise strong recruiting videos:

- **Excessive slow-motion** — show the play at full speed. A short slow-motion replay can help point out a detail
- **Inappropriate music** — if the video has audio, keep it professional
- **Heavy editing effects** — flashy transitions, color filters and graphics can get in the way of the play. Clean, simple edits keep the focus on your son
- **Long career retrospectives** — recent footage shows how he plays now
- **Out-of-date material** — older clips show how he used to play
- **Filler clips** — any clip that doesn't actively showcase a strength is detracting from the video
- **Mistakes that aren't acknowledged** — a missed shot or a turnover in the middle of a video raises questions

## Position-specific footage approach

**For attackers, midfielders, and other field players**: Build the video around dynamic offensive plays. Lead with scoring, work in feeds and assists, show dodging and stick skills, demonstrate transition play, and conclude with ride and defensive contributions.

**For LSMs**: Build the video around ground balls, transition play, and defensive midfield work. The LSM position is hybrid, and coaches need to see both defensive technique and offensive transition contributions.

**For FOGOs**: Build the video around faceoff competition. Include close-up footage of faceoff mechanics where possible. Then add ground ball play after wins, athletic ability when faceoffs don't go as planned, and any field play (ask coaches whether they want a FOGO who stays on the field after the faceoff).

**For defenders**: Build the video around one-versus-one defense, takeaways, caused turnovers, and transition play. Stick work with the long pole — particularly clears and outlet passes — should be featured.

**For goalies**: Build the video around saves of varying difficulty and shot types, clearing-game ability, and athletic positioning. Communication and leadership should be visible even in highlight clips.

## When to update the video

Practical guidance on update frequency:

- **End of high school season**: Update with the strongest plays from the recent season
- **End of summer club season**: Add standout clips from summer tournaments
- **Before fall recruiting events**: Refresh with the most recent material to send to coaches you'll see at fall events
- **Whenever you have meaningful new footage**: If you have 1-2 minutes of new content stronger than what's in your current video, refresh

## Technical considerations

- **Resolution**: Shoot in 1080p or 4K if possible
- **Camera position**: An elevated view from midfield shows more of the field
- **Avoid heavy zoom**: Wide-angle shots that show the field around the play give a coach context that tight zoom loses
- **Audio quality**: Ensure any included audio is clear

## The pre-contact-date reality

Per the 2026-27 NCAA Division I Men's Lacrosse Recruiting Calendar (published directly by the NCAA at `ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com`) and discussed in detail in [The September 1 Junior Year Rule](/lacrosse/guide/september-1-junior-year-rule/), D1 college coaches cannot initiate substantive recruiting communication before the start of an athlete's junior year. For men's lacrosse, effective August 1, 2026, that moment is 7 a.m. on the Wednesday immediately following Labor Day — not September 1. The restriction includes responding substantively to athlete-initiated outreach.

What this means for recruiting video:

- **Before the contact date**: You can still send videos to coaches. Coaches can watch them, save them, and add the athlete to their tracking. Coaches cannot respond substantively beyond limited communication.
- **Have the video ready before the contact date**: coaches can watch it before they are allowed to respond, so a current video is in place when contact opens.
- **From the contact date on**, Division I coaches can initiate contact and extend offers, so keep the video current through junior and senior year.

## Special considerations for Canadian recruits

For Canadian men's lacrosse recruits considering NCAA programs (see [International Recruiting in Men's College Lacrosse](/lacrosse/mens/guide/international-recruiting/) for the broader context):

- Include both box and field lacrosse footage if both are part of your athletic profile
- Ask each coach whether they want box lacrosse footage, and what field footage they want alongside it
- If your primary experience is box lacrosse, field lacrosse footage shows a coach how you play the NCAA game
- Footage from Ontario Junior Lacrosse League games, BC Lacrosse Association events, and other Canadian competition can be used

## How to share the video

Practical sharing guidance:

- **Hosting platform**: Hudl and YouTube are both options; ask the coaches you're contacting which they prefer
- **Privacy settings**: Allow direct link access (unlisted or password-protected — not public, and not strictly invitation-only)
- **Sharing format**: Put a direct link in your email rather than attaching the video file
- **One link, easily found**: Don't make coaches search for the video
- **Coach's email subject line**: make it specific — for example, "2027 Attacker, [Name] — Highlights Inside" — rather than a generic introduction

## Every recruit's video journey is different

The recruiting video is a tool, not a guarantee. Some recruits with a simple video find their fit through tournament play and direct outreach; some with a polished video take longer to find the right program. The video is one piece of a recruiting puzzle that also includes academic performance, athletic development, character, communication skills, family financial situation, and dozens of other factors. Use this guide as context. Build a video that honestly represents your son's current ability. And remember that a coach who is interested in your son may want to see him play in person — the video's job is to get him on coaches' lists, not to substitute for everything else in the recruiting process.
