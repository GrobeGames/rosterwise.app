If your athlete is new to rowing, or you are, the first conversation with a college coach can sound like another language. Boats have symbols, sides have names, and seats have numbers. None of it is hard once it is defined, and all of it comes up in recruiting.

This page defines the terms using USRowing's own terminology and its 2026 Rules of Rowing, the national rule book, and World Rowing's own pages. The championship details come from the NCAA's own 2025-26 championship manuals.

## Sweep rowing and sculling

USRowing's rule book defines rowing with a fact that explains a lot of the vocabulary: in rowing, **the athletes sit with their backs to the direction the boat is moving**.

From there, rowing splits into two kinds:

- **Sweep rowing.** Each rower uses **one oar**. The sweep boats are the **pair, the four and the eight**.
- **Sculling.** Each rower uses **two oars, one in each hand**. The sculling boats are the **single, the double and the quad**.

When a coach, a roster or a results sheet says "sweep" or "sculling," this is the distinction.

## Boat classes and their symbols

World Rowing writes each boat class as a short symbol.

| Boat | Symbol | Sweep or sculling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single sculls | 1x | Sculling |
| Double sculls | 2x | Sculling |
| Quadruple sculls | 4x | Sculling |
| Pair | 2- | Sweep |
| Coxed pair | 2+ | Sweep |
| Four | 4- | Sweep |
| Coxed four | 4+ | Sweep |
| Eight | 8+ | Sweep |

**The + and - signs are about the coxswain.** In USRowing's terminology, **"+" means the boat carries a coxswain and "-" means it doesn't**. The eight is written 8+: **eight rowers and a coxswain**. A coxless four (4-) is often called a "straight four." USRowing also notes that a quad (4x) can be rowed with or without a coxswain.

## Port and starboard

USRowing defines **port** as the **left side of the boat when facing forward, toward the bow**, and **starboard** as the **right side**. A "port" or a "starboard" is a sweep rower whose oar is on that side of the boat. World Rowing uses other names for the same sides: it calls port **"strokeside"** and starboard **"bowside."**

Because rowers sit facing backward, the sides are named for the boat, not for the rower. A port rower's oar is on the boat's left, which is on the rower's own right as they sit.

## Seat numbers, from bow to stroke

USRowing defines a seat number as "A rower's position in the boat counting from the bow." Two seats have names:

- **Bow.** The bow seat is the rower closest to the bow.
- **Stroke.** The stroke is the rower closest to the stern, and **sets the stroke rate and rhythm** for the crew.

In an eight, USRowing calls **seats 6, 5, 4 and 3** the "engine room."

## The coxswain

The **coxswain** steers the boat and is responsible for **safety, motivating the crew and race strategy**, but does not row. That is USRowing's definition, in its terminology and in its rule book.

In the NCAA's 2025-26 championship manuals, **every championship boat carries a coxswain: the eight (8+) and the four (4+)**. Coxswains also have their own weight rules, set separately by each governing body. Our [coxswain recruiting guide](/rowing/guide/coxswain-recruiting/) covers them.

## 2,000-meter races and fall head races

**The standard race distance is 2,000 meters.** World Rowing notes that it has been the Olympic distance since **1912**, and that women switched from 1,000 m to 2,000 m before the **1988** Games. USRowing's course standard is also **2,000 m** from start to finish. Under the NCAA's 2025-26 championship manuals, every event at the NCAA women's championships, in all three divisions, is raced over 2,000 meters.

A **head race** is different. USRowing's rule book defines it as a **race against the clock**: crews start at **different times**, and the order of finish is decided by elapsed time. USRowing's terminology describes it as "a long race, usually about 3 miles long (held in the fall)."

One Division I selection rule now touches fall racing directly. At its June 15-16, 2026 meeting, the NCAA's Division I Women's Rowing Oversight Committee decided that, **starting in 2026-27, fall races no longer count toward the minimum number of races needed for at-large selection**.

## Which boats race and score at the NCAA championships

The NCAA's rowing championships are the **NCAA Divisions I, II and III Women's Rowing Championships**, held on the same weekend at the same site. The events and points below are the ones the NCAA's **2025-26 prechampionship manuals** set for the **2026 championships**. Formats can change from year to year, so check the current manual.

**The NCAA championships have no novice event.** The word "novice" does not appear in any of the three 2025-26 championship manuals.

### Division I

Division I teams race **three events, each over 2,000 meters: the I Eights, the II Eights and the Fours.** Every team must field **two eights and one four**. Rowers may not race in two boats at the championship, and there are no pairs.

Every boat's finish scores points for its team:

| Event | Points for 1st place | Each place below drops by | Points for 22nd place |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I Eights | 66 | 3 | 3 |
| II Eights | 44 | 2 | 2 |
| Fours | 22 | 1 | 1 |

A tie for a team award is broken by the I Eights place.

### Division II

Division II has **no more than 6 teams**, racing **two events: an Eight and a Four, each with a coxswain, over 2,000 meters.**

| Event | Points, 1st through 6th |
| --- | --- |
| Eights | 18, 15, 12, 9, 6, 3 |
| Fours | 12, 10, 8, 6, 4, 2 |

A tie is broken by the Eights finish.

### Division III

Division III has **8 teams**, racing **two events: the I Eight and the II Eight, over 2,000 meters.**

| Event | Points for 1st place | Each place below drops by | Points for 8th place |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I Eights | 40 | 3 | 19 |
| II Eights | 16 | 2 | 2 |

In every division, a team's top eight (the I Eight, or Division II's one Eight) scores more points than any of its other boats at the same place.

### Men's crews and lightweight women: the IRA's events

**The NCAA has no men's rowing championship.** In its 2026-27 Division I, II and III Manuals, rowing appears only in the women's column of the championship lists. The Intercollegiate Rowing Association's constitution describes its **IRA National Championship** as a post-season national championship for the top intercollegiate **heavyweight men's, lightweight men's and lightweight women's** crews. The IRA regatta also includes a **Division 3** championship. Our guide to [who governs college rowing](/rowing/guide/governing-bodies/) explains how the bodies fit together.

The IRA's undated general-information page, which lists dates for 2027 to 2029, gives these events:

- **Heavyweight men:** Varsity 8+, 2nd Varsity 8+, 3rd Varsity 8+ and Varsity 4+.
- **Lightweight men:** Varsity 8+, 2nd Varsity 8+ and Coxed 4+.
- **Lightweight women:** Varsity 8+, Coxed 4+ and Double (2x).
- **Division 3:** Varsity 8+ and 2nd Varsity 8+.

## Questions to ask coaches

1. **"Do you see my athlete as a sweep rower, a sculler, or both?"** The answer tells you how the program pictures your athlete's development.
2. **"Does it matter for your lineup whether my athlete rows port or starboard?"** Only the coach knows what the program's lineup needs.
3. **"Which boats does your program race in the spring, and which of them count toward the championship you aim for?"** The boats that score are set by the championship your program races at, not by a general rule.
4. **"How much of your fall season is head racing?"** It tells you what the fall looks like for a first-year rower.
5. **"Which boat would a first-year rower in your program race in?"** It turns a general conversation about the roster into a specific one about the boat.

## The words are fixed; the boats are not

The vocabulary on this page does not change from season to season. The championship formats can. The events and points here are the ones the NCAA's 2025-26 manuals set for the 2026 championships, and the IRA's events are those its general-information page listed in October 2026. Before your athlete's season, check the current NCAA manual and the IRA's own pages, and ask each coach how their crews are lined up.
