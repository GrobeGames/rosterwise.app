## What the House settlement actually is

The House settlement resolves three lawsuits brought by college athletes — *House*, *Hubbard* and *Carter* — against the NCAA and five conferences: the Atlantic Coast Conference, the Big Ten, the Big 12, the Pac-12 and the Southeastern Conference, known in the settlement as the defendant conferences. The court granted final approval on June 6, 2025, and implementation began July 1, 2025.

**Two things happened:**

1. **A backward-looking component:** a damages award to the athlete classes in the three cases. The lawyers representing the athlete classes publish how it is allocated, at [collegeathletecompensation.com](https://www.collegeathletecompensation.com).
2. **A forward-looking component:** a new system in Division I that lets schools pay athletes directly, with roster limits in every sport and without the old sport-by-sport limits on athletic aid.

The forward-looking changes are what matter for families recruiting now. They change how athletic aid works and how many athletes a Division I roster may hold.

## The three biggest changes

### 1. Revenue sharing

A Division I school that takes part in the settlement — a Participating Institution — may now provide payments and benefits directly to its athletes, up to an annual benefits cap. The NCAA's implementation Q&A lists what counts against the cap: the school's payments to athletes for the use of their name, image and likeness; up to $2.5 million a year of athletic aid above the old 2024-25 team limits; and up to $2.5 million of academic and graduation (Alston) awards. Payments from third parties do not count (Questions D7 and D9).

The key numbers:

- **$20.5 million per school** was the cap for the **2025-26** academic year — the settlement's first year, and the most recent figure in the NCAA's implementation Q&A.
- The cap is **recalculated every three years** from the settlement's formula. In the second and third year of each three-year period it **increases four percent** from the previous year. It is not a flat four percent every year, and a recalculation year can move it by more or less.
- The cap is set at **22%** of the averaged athletic revenue of the five defendant conferences plus Notre Dame, totaled across eight NCAA financial-reporting categories.
- **The NCAA's implementation Q&A (updated February 11, 2026) gives no 2026-27 dollar figure.** If revenue sharing matters to your family's decision, ask each program directly what its current pool is and what share of it goes to your athlete's sport.
- Each school decides how to divide the money among its sports: the NCAA's implementation Q&A caps the school's total and lets it provide benefits "at its discretion" within that cap, with no sport-by-sport split.

Revenue sharing is separate from third-party NIL deals. The school's own payments are made for the use of an athlete's name, image and likeness; a third-party NIL deal is a payment from anyone other than the school or an entity it owns or controls, and every Division I athlete must report one worth $600 or more to NIL Go, the College Sports Commission's reporting platform.

**What this means for recruits:** a Division I program may now include payments in an offer. Ask what is offered, for how many years, and on what conditions.

### 2. Hard roster limits

Before the settlement, Division I sports had limits on athletic aid but no limit on roster size. A men's soccer program might have had 9.9 equivalency scholarships while carrying additional athletes as walk-ons, because nothing limited the size of the roster itself.

The settlement replaced that system. Division I schools in the five defendant conferences, and those that opt in, now have **roster limits** in every sport (Bylaw 17.2). After a sport's roster deadline, anyone who takes part in practice or other athletically related activities must be on the submitted roster, and the limit counts everyone on it, with or without aid — apart from designated student-athletes (below) and the other exceptions Bylaw 17.2.2 lists, such as for a season-ending injury or a student manager.

How much a limit changes a particular program depends on how many players it carried before; ask.

### 3. Scholarship restructuring

The old sport-specific aid limits — for example 9.9 equivalencies in men's soccer, 14 in women's soccer, and 12 head-count scholarships in women's volleyball — are gone: "the Division I Manual no longer includes institutional financial aid limits for any sport" (NCAA House Q&A, Question B2). A Participating Institution may give "any amount of an athletics scholarship (up to cost of attendance) to any student-athlete" on a sport's submitted roster.

Aid above a sport's old 2024-25 limit counts against the school's benefits cap, up to $2.5 million a year (Question D9). Within that, programs can fund more athletes at higher levels — or concentrate money on fewer athletes. The decision is made by each school, so two programs in the same conference may award aid very differently.

For a deeper look at how scholarships work under this new structure, see our [athletic scholarships guide](/guide/athletic-scholarships/).

**These numbers are the ceiling, not the floor.** A program may carry fewer athletes than its limit.

**There was no roster limit before.** Until July 1, 2025, Division I sports had limits on athletic aid but none on roster size, so a program could carry more players than it funded. The limits above are the first cap on how many players a Division I roster may hold.

## What happened to scholarship caps

Under the old system, each sport had specific scholarship limits. Men's soccer: 9.9 equivalencies. Women's soccer: 14 equivalencies. Baseball: 11.7 equivalencies. These limits capped how much athletic aid a program could award and how it could be divided.

Those limits are no longer in the Division I Manual. At a Participating Institution, how much athletic aid each sport receives is the school's decision.

**What this means in practice:**

- A school could fund every athlete on a soccer roster at 100% scholarship — or fund them all at 50%. No per-sport limit caps the decision; the old 2024-25 limit now marks where aid starts to count against the school's benefits cap (Questions D9 and D10).
- A Division I school outside the settlement may give athletic aid up to the old 2024-25 limits without opting in — in a former head-count sport, up to their value measured in equivalencies (NCAA House Q&A, Questions B2 and B4).
- **Families cannot assume scholarship amounts based on old equivalency numbers.** The only way to know what a specific program is offering is to ask.

For families, understanding a program's scholarship offer now takes a direct conversation — the old rules of thumb no longer apply.

## The DSA transition provision

The settlement included a transition provision called Designated Student-Athlete (DSA) status, for athletes who would otherwise have lost their roster spot when the limits took effect.

A DSA is an athlete the school attests "was or would have been removed from the institution's 2025-26 roster due to the implementation of roster limits," and who was on the 2024-25 roster before April 7, 2025, or was recruited before that date. Schools that opted in for 2025-26 submitted their DSA lists by July 6, 2025. A DSA on a sport's submitted roster does not count toward its roster limit for as long as the athlete has eligibility remaining (Bylaw 17.2.2.2), so a sport may exceed its limit by its number of DSAs.

**What this means now:** DSA status belongs to athletes who were on rosters, or recruited, before April 7, 2025. A high school recruit arriving now counts against the roster limit.

The DSA provision matters for families with athletes already in college, but for incoming recruits, the practical takeaway is simple: the roster limits are the roster limits.

## Impact on walk-ons

Under the old system, 9.9 equivalencies could not fund a full scholarship for every athlete on a roster, and no rule capped how many additional athletes a program carried. Under the new system the roster limit itself is the binding constraint. Across the {% stat "soccer.2025-26.m.d1.roster_size", "programs" %} Division I men's soccer programs RosterWise analyzed for the 2025-26 season — the first season played under the new limits — the median roster was exactly {% stat "soccer.2025-26.m.d1.roster_size", "median" %} players, matching the cap, with the middle half of programs between {% stat "soccer.2025-26.m.d1.roster_size", "p25" %} and {% stat "soccer.2025-26.m.d1.roster_size", "p75" %}.

So at a Division I program under the limits, a walk-on needs one of the same fixed number of roster spots as a scholarship player. If your athlete was planning a walk-on path at a Division I program, ask the coach directly whether a spot exists for a player without aid.

For a detailed breakdown of the different types of walk-ons, see our [walk-on guide](/guide/walk-on-types/).

## Impact on the transfer portal

**Every roster spot counts.** At a Division I program under the limits, a transfer who joins takes one of its fixed number of spots, the same as an incoming freshman. Ask a program how many spots it expects to fill from the portal in the year your athlete would arrive.

**Payments can be part of a transfer decision too.** An athlete's revenue-sharing terms at one school and an offer from another are something to compare, in the same way as scholarship offers.

For more on how the transfer portal works and how to evaluate its impact on a program, see our [transfer portal guide](/guide/transfer-portal/).

## Schools that opted in vs. opted out

Division I schools are not all in the same position, and for members of the five defendant conferences it was never a choice.

**Who is bound, and who chooses.** Per the NCAA's "Q&A: Implementation of the House Settlement" (published June 13, 2025), "any Division I institution that is a member of a defendant conference (i.e., the Atlantic Coast Conference, the Big Ten Conference, the Big 12 Conference, the Pac-12 Conference, and the Southeastern Conference) or is providing additional payments or benefits is a Participating Institution." Members of those five conferences are bound as defendants — they did not "opt in." Every other Division I school chooses.

**And the choice can change from year to year.** For 2025-26, non-defendant institutions had to formally opt in by June 30, 2025. For 2026-27, the NCAA's current Q&A says only that "information regarding the 2026-27 processes for opting-in/opting-out will be circulated by the College Sports Commission no later than March 1, 2026." A school may opt in for the next academic year at any point in the settlement's ten-year term, and a school that opted in may "return to awarding benefits at pre-settlement levels" (Questions A8 and A9).

**We do not publish a count of how many schools opted in.** The figures that circulate come from secondary reporting rather than an NCAA-published list, and because a school's choice can change from year to year they go stale. Since a school's status can change between the year your athlete is recruited and the year they enrol, **ask the program directly whether it has opted in for the year your athlete would arrive** — and ask again if the recruitment spans more than one cycle.

### What being a Participating Institution means

- The school may provide payments and benefits directly to athletes, up to the annual cap
- The school must comply with the Bylaw 17.2 roster limits
- The school decides how much athletic aid each sport receives; no sport-specific limit applies

### What staying outside the settlement means

For a Division I school outside the five defendant conferences that does not opt in:

- The school does not provide the settlement's payments and benefits
- It may give athletic aid up to the old 2024-25 limits (in a former head-count sport, their value in equivalencies); giving more brings it under the settlement (NCAA House Q&A, Questions B2, B4 and A16)
- Roster size "may be governed by the conference or institution and not legislated at the national level" — unless the school gives athletic aid above the 2024-25 Division I limits, which brings it under the settlement, roster limits included (NCAA House Q&A, Question A16)

**What this means for families:** You need to know whether a school your athlete is considering is a Participating Institution. The answer changes how scholarships work, how many athletes are on the roster, and what financial package might be available. Ask directly. Do not assume.

## What this means for high school recruits

If your athlete is in high school and being recruited for college athletics, here is what the House settlement changes about your experience:

**Every roster spot is counted.** At a Division I program under the limits, your athlete needs one of a fixed number of spots — 28 in soccer, 18 in volleyball, 48 in men's lacrosse.

**Scholarship conversations are different.** The old framework of "this sport gets X equivalencies" is gone. Ask each program what it is offering and how it compares to what it offered before the settlement. There is no standard template anymore.

**Revenue sharing is a new factor.** A program may include revenue-sharing payments in an offer, and the NCAA publishes no split of the cap among sports, so what a program offers is the school's decision. Treat revenue-sharing offers the way you treat any financial commitment — get specifics, understand the terms, and compare across programs.

**Walk-on paths depend on the program.** If your athlete was planning to walk on at a Division I program, ask whether a roster spot exists for a player without aid. Division II, Division III and NAIA programs are not under Bylaw 17.2, and the NAIA sets no roster limit at all.

**Ask more questions.** The settlement has made questions about roster composition, scholarship terms, and playing-time expectations more important, not less. See our [contacting coaches guide](/guide/contacting-coaches/) for advice on communicating with coaching staff.

**Understand NIL and revenue sharing honestly.** See our [NIL and revenue sharing guide](/guide/nil-and-revenue-sharing/) for what the rules say about each, and what to ask a program.

**Understand how aid stacks.** See our [stacking financial aid guide](/guide/stacking-financial-aid/) for how athletic, academic and need-based aid fit together.

## What this means for D2, D3, and NAIA

The settlement's terms apply in Division I: the NCAA's implementation Q&A is guidance "to the Division I membership." Division II, Division III and NAIA schools work under their own rules.

### Division II

Every Division II sport remains an equivalency sport under Division II's own aid limits (Bylaw 15.4.2).

### Division III

D3 offers no athletic scholarships — Division III institutions may not award financial aid on the basis of athletics — and is built on a different model entirely.

### NAIA

The NAIA has its own rules: a limit on the institutional aid each sport may award its athletes (12 equivalencies per soccer team, for example) and no roster limit at all.

**The bottom line for non-D1 divisions:** look at each on its own terms. Their roster and aid rules differ from Division I's, as above, and from each other's.

## What is still uncertain

The settlement took effect July 1, 2025; 2026-27 is its second academic year. Its implementation is ongoing, and the NCAA's implementation Q&A has been updated since — most recently in the February 11, 2026 edition.

**How schools divide the cap is their own decision.** The NCAA publishes no allocation among sports, so what a program offers can differ from school to school and from year to year.

**The roster limits have already been revised once.** Bylaw 17.2 carries a revision dated August 25, 2026: a women's flag football limit effective from 2027-28. Check the current Division I Manual for the limit in your athlete's sport.

**The NCAA's implementation Q&A is updated as it goes.** The February 11, 2026 edition marks its changes. Ask a program which rules it is working under for the year your athlete would arrive.

**Families should not assume that the current system is the permanent system.** It is the current system. Stay informed, ask questions, and verify facts before making decisions.

## Practical takeaways for families

**1. Ask every Division I program whether it is a Participating Institution for the year your athlete would arrive.** The answer changes how scholarships, roster limits, and revenue sharing work at that school. Do not assume.

**2. Understand the roster limit for your athlete's sport.** Know how many spots exist and ask how many athletes are currently on the roster. A program at or near its limit has less room than one with open spots.

**3. Get specifics on scholarship offers.** The old rules of thumb are gone. Ask what the program is offering, how it compares to what they offered before the settlement, and what revenue-sharing payments (if any) are part of the package.

**4. Know the rules in each division you're considering.** D2, D3 and NAIA programs work under different roster and aid rules from Division I's, as above.

**5. Don't assume a walk-on spot at a Division I program.** If walking on was the plan, have an honest conversation with the coaching staff about whether a spot realistically exists.

**6. Factor revenue sharing into financial comparisons — carefully.** Revenue-sharing payments are new and their terms may change. Before you rely on a first-year revenue-sharing offer, ask whether it is guaranteed, how it might change, and what conditions apply.

**7. Stay current.** This landscape is evolving. Check NCAA.org for the latest rules. Ask programs for current information, not what was true last year. What you read six months ago may already be outdated.

**8. Keep the fit questions.** The settlement changed the financial and structural framework of Division I athletics. It did not change what a family has to work out about each program: whether it fits your athlete athletically, academically, socially, and financially.
