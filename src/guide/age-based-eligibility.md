## First: "5 in 5" is a nickname, and it's misleading

You'll hear this called the **"5 in 5" rule** — five seasons in five years (you'll also see it written "5 for 5"). That shorthand is how most families first encounter it, so we use it here. But the NCAA itself says that calling it the "5 for 5" rule is **"not accurate,"** and the difference can cost an athlete a year.

The NCAA's own name for it is the **age-based eligibility model**. It does **not** hand every athlete five seasons. It gives an athlete a **five-year window**, and that window can start *before they ever set foot on a college campus*. For an athlete who enrolls full time straight out of high school, the window opens at enrollment and five seasons in five years is possible. For an athlete who takes a gap year, a prep year, a junior-college stop, or arrives from an international pathway, it can be considerably less than five usable seasons.

If you read nothing else on this page, read the section on [when your clock starts](#when-your-five-year-clock-starts).

## What actually changed

Before June 2026, under NCAA Bylaw 12.6, Division I athletes could compete in **four seasons of competition within five years** of full-time enrollment. That four-season cap is what made "redshirting" — sitting out a season so it doesn't count as one of the four — possible. Division II's version was four seasons of competition within **10 semesters or 15 quarters**.

The NCAA has replaced both with a model built on a single five-year period. Per the NCAA, Division I athletes can have **up to five years of eligibility** if they enroll full time no later than the academic year after their 19th birthday, and the limit on seasons of competition is gone.

**Division I** adopted the model in **June 2026**. The NCAA's release announcing it is dated **June 23, 2026** and describes a unanimous Division I Cabinet vote that was not final until the Cabinet's meeting concluded the next day — which is why you'll see both June 23 and June 24 cited in news coverage.

**Division II** followed. The NCAA announced on **August 5, 2026** that the Division II Executive Board had adopted the model as emergency legislation, **effective immediately for the 2026-27 academic year**. The NCAA's Eligibility 101 page now covers both divisions.

## When your five-year clock starts

This is the heart of the rule. Under the age-based model, an athlete's five-year period of eligibility begins at **whichever of these happens first**:

1. **First full-time enrollment** — the academic term when the athlete first enrolls full time and attends class at **any** college or university. The NCAA spells out that this includes a domestic school, an **international institution**, or a **two-year college**; **or**
2. **The age trigger** — the start of the regular academic year immediately following the athlete's **19th birthday**. For an athlete who turns 19 on or after September 1, the NCAA says the period begins at "the start of the subsequent academic year," unless the athlete enrolls full time earlier.

And then the part families most often miss. In the NCAA's words, once the period starts it runs continuously — it "does not pause because a student-athlete does not compete, transfers, sits out, changes teams or takes time away from participation".

The clock is not a bank of seasons you spend. It's a window that opens and then keeps running.

## What disappeared: redshirts and most waivers

The model deliberately simplifies a large section of the rulebook. According to the NCAA's Eligibility 101 page, it eliminates:

- **Seasons-of-competition limits**
- **Athletics redshirt rules**
- **Delayed-enrollment / organized-competition rules**
- **Sport-specific enrollment timelines**
- **Academic nonqualifier season limits** (Division I)
- **The 10-semester / 15-quarter limit** (Division II)

It also eliminates several familiar waiver categories: **medical hardship waivers**, **extension-of-eligibility waivers**, season-of-competition waivers, athletics activity waivers, and delayed-enrollment / organized-competition waivers.

This is a genuine shift in how families think about planning. Under the old system, a lost season to injury was something you sought a hardship waiver to recover. Under the age-based model, there is no such waiver — the five-year window simply continues to run.

That applies to current athletes too. The NCAA says athletes who continue under the previous rules will not have future waiver opportunities in the eliminated categories. In Division I, waiver requests under the old rules, based on circumstances during or before 2025-26, were due by **July 31, 2026**.

### The exceptions that remain

A few circumstances can still keep time from counting against the window:

- **Service.** Time in **active-duty military service**, on an **official religious mission**, or in "a similar service commitment" can be excluded — but only if the athlete does not take part in organized competition during that time.
- **Pregnancy.** A pregnant student-athlete may pause the period for the **actual time she cannot compete**, with medical documentation.

The NCAA Eligibility Center administers these exceptions.

## Who it applies to — and when

The transition is staged, and which group your athlete falls into matters. Per the NCAA:

- **Athletes who used their final season of competition (under the previous rules) in 2025-26:** **no additional eligibility.**
- **Current athletes with eligibility remaining after 2025-26:** evaluated under whichever rules — the previous rules or the age-based model — are **more beneficial** for that individual.
- **Athletes first enrolling full time in 2026-27:** also whichever rules are more beneficial.
- **Athletes first enrolling full time in fall 2027 or later:** the **age-based model only**.

That "more beneficial" provision is the bridge between the two systems, and it's why some current athletes gain a season while others see no change. It is also why the court case below exists: the first group on that list, athletes who finished their fourth season in 2025-26, is the group the transition leaves out.

## The delayed-enrollment trap

This is the section we'd most want a recruiting family to understand, because it inverts advice that has been standard for years.

The NCAA says it plainly: the rule "does not guarantee five years for all student-athletes, particularly those who delay enrollment," and "Delaying college enrollment beyond age 19 may reduce the amount of eligibility available in Divisions I and II."

Because the clock starts at the **earlier** of enrollment or the academic year after an athlete turns 19 — and because it **never pauses** — time spent *not* enrolled after that trigger is time spent burning the window. Practically, that affects:

- **Gap years and post-graduate/prep years.** A year taken to develop, reclassify, or re-open recruiting no longer sits outside the system. If the age trigger has passed, it consumes part of the five-year window.
- **Athletes who reclassify or start school later.** Anyone whose birthday and school timeline put them past the age trigger before enrollment starts with less runway.
- **International and academy pathways.** Full-time enrollment at an international college starts the clock, and any pathway that keeps an athlete out of full-time enrollment past the age trigger uses window time.
- **Religious missions and military service** are the notable carve-outs — see the exceptions above.

We want to be careful here: the NCAA's published rule establishes the triggers and the fact that the clock does not pause. The consequence for late enrollees follows directly from those two facts. But how any individual athlete's window is calculated is a determination for the **NCAA Eligibility Center** and the school's compliance office — not something to estimate from a web page, including this one.

## What this means if you're considering junior college

The age-based clock starts at first full-time enrollment at **any** college or university, and the NCAA's guidance names **two-year colleges** explicitly. Division II's announcement says the same, naming junior colleges.

That doesn't make the JUCO route a bad one; it makes the *timing* of it something to plan deliberately rather than assume. An athlete who enrolls full time at a two-year college has started the same five-year window they'd have started at a four-year school. The strategic question is what the years after that look like, and whether the transfer lands where the athlete wants to be.

This is also an area of active litigation. A federal court order from October 2, 2026 describes a group of pending lawsuits that challenge the NCAA bylaws counting seasons of competition at junior colleges and NAIA schools against an athlete's NCAA eligibility. Those cases are unresolved.

If you're weighing that path, our [junior college (JUCO) guide](/guide/njcaa-juco-explained/) covers how the NJCAA works, what transferring on actually requires, and — importantly — which outcome claims are and aren't backed by verified data.

## The court fight, and why the ground is still moving

The rule as adopted has been challenged in court. Here is what the court records show, without taking a side.

**July 31, 2026 — a class-wide preliminary injunction.** In *Wisne v. NCAA*, U.S. District Judge Charlotte N. Sweeney of the District of Colorado certified a class and granted a preliminary injunction. The court defines the class as athletes in the United States who **began to play college sports in the 2022-23 season**, competed in NCAA Division I, and **completed four years of eligibility under the NCAA's prior rules by the end of the 2025-26 season**. The injunction barred the NCAA from stopping class members from competing in a fifth season. The claim is brought under Section 1 of the Sherman Act, a federal antitrust law. At this early stage the judge found the plaintiffs "likely to succeed on the merits" of that claim — a preliminary finding, not a final ruling.

**August 2, 2026 — a clarification of what the injunction does not cover.** At the NCAA's request, Judge Sweeney issued an order spelling out the injunction's limits:

- It **does not block NCAA transfer rules**.
- It **does not reach any provision of the House v. NCAA settlement**, and the order names **roster caps** among the things it did not enjoin.
- It does not stop a school from making eligibility decisions based on whether an athlete was **over 20 when they enrolled in 2022-23**.

**August 2 to 10 — an appeal, and a stay denied.** The same day, the NCAA filed a notice of appeal to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit and asked Judge Sweeney to pause (stay) her injunction while the appeal proceeds. She denied that request on August 10.

**August 21 — the Tenth Circuit stayed the injunction.** A three-judge Tenth Circuit panel granted the NCAA's request and stayed the July 31 injunction "pending the disposition of this appeal on the merits, or until further order of this court." The order is two pages and gives no reasoning beyond finding that the NCAA met the legal test for a stay. One judge on the panel, Judge Rossman, would have denied it. In a letter to schools afterward, NCAA Chief Legal Officer Scott Bearby said the age-based rules would be implemented, effective immediately, "as the Division I membership intended," according to reporting by Dan Murphy published by TSN.

What that means today: **while the stay is in place, the fifth season the injunction allowed is not in effect.**

**October 13 — oral argument.** The appeal has been fully briefed, and the Tenth Circuit has set oral argument for **October 13, 2026**. As of October 8, there is no ruling on the merits of the appeal. The underlying case in Colorado is also unresolved; a preliminary injunction is never a final judgment.

**Other federal lawsuits stay in their own courts.** The NCAA asked the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation to gather the federal eligibility cases into one court. On **October 2, 2026** the panel denied that request, calling it "a close question." Its order counts seven actions in five districts, plus fifteen related actions in nine districts. Those cases continue separately.

## Where this collides with roster limits

One practical consequence deserves its own note. At Division I schools in the [House v. NCAA settlement](/guide/house-settlement/), each sport now has a **roster limit**. The August 2 clarification confirmed the injunction did **not** lift those roster caps — and the injunction itself is now stayed.

The squeeze doesn't depend on the court case, though. Under the age-based model, the cap on seasons of competition is gone, and current athletes with eligibility remaining are evaluated under whichever rules are more favorable to them. More eligibility at the top of a roster, with a fixed number of roster spots, is the combination incoming recruits are walking into. We're not going to put a number on that effect — no verified data exists — but it's the right question to ask a coach directly.

## The federal bill in Congress

On **September 28, 2026**, the U.S. Senate passed **S. 4668, the Protect College Sports Act of 2026**, by a vote of **77-22**. As passed by the Senate, the bill would write a five-year eligibility window into federal law for **Division I and Division II**: a maximum of five calendar years, beginning at the earlier of the academic year after an athlete's 19th birthday or initial full-time enrollment.

One difference in the text is worth knowing. Besides pregnancy, religious missions, and active-duty military service, the bill would let an association adopt uniform rules that stop the clock for other absences, which "may include serious athletic injury or medical condition." The NCAA's current model eliminated medical hardship waivers and has no injury exception.

The House received the bill on **October 5, 2026**. **It is not law**, and it does not change the NCAA's rules today.

## What we don't know yet

In keeping with how we handle every fast-moving topic:

- **How the Tenth Circuit will rule.** Argument is October 13, 2026. The injunction is stayed until the court decides the appeal or orders otherwise. We won't predict the outcome.
- **Division II: settled.** The Division II Executive Board **adopted** the age-based model, announced August 5, 2026, effective immediately for 2026-27. The clock starts the same way, and the transition follows the same structure: no additional eligibility for athletes whose eligibility ran out under the old rules, the more favorable model for current athletes and 2026-27 enrollees, and the age-based model only from fall 2027.
- **Division III: proposed, not adopted.** A Division III membership proposal, sponsored by the American Rivers Conference and the Ohio Athletic Conference and submitted June 30, 2026, is filed for the **2027 NCAA Convention (January 13–15, 2027)**. As written, it would apply to students who first enroll full time in 2027-28 or later and would allow no waivers of the five-year period. It has not been voted on.
- **NAIA: no change found.** NAIA's own page still describes **four seasons of competition during an athlete's first 10 semesters**, with no age-based model.
- **NJCAA: not verified.** We could not verify an NJCAA position. Anyone telling you definitively how the age-based model treats NJCAA athletes should be asked for a first-party source.
- **The effect on recruiting volume or roster spots.** No first-party data exists. Be skeptical of anyone quantifying it.

## What families should actually do now

1. **Separate what's settled from what isn't.** The age-based model is in force in Division I and Division II. What's under appeal is whether one group of athletes, those who started college in 2022-23, gets a fifth season. A gap year, a reclassification, or a prep year is a real commitment, and it would be made under the settled part of the rules.
2. **Get your athlete's specific window confirmed in writing** by the NCAA Eligibility Center or a compliance officer — not from a forum, a highlight-tape service, or this page.
3. **Ask coaches directly** how they're reading the interaction between returning athletes with extra eligibility and roster limits for the class you're in.
4. **Re-check dates before you act.** Everything here is verified as of October 8, 2026, and the Tenth Circuit hears argument on October 13.

The underlying recruiting question hasn't changed, though. Eligibility rules determine *how long* an athlete can compete; they don't tell you **where your athlete fits**. That still comes down to who's on the roster, what they play, when they graduate, and whether the school is a place your athlete would want to be even if the sport ended tomorrow.
