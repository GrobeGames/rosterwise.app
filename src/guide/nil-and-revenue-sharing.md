## What changed in 2024-2025

Here's the timeline:

**October 2024:** The NCAA Division I Council eliminated the National Letter of Intent (NLI) for Division I, replacing it with the written offer of athletics aid. NCAA.org states that Division II schools may still use the NLI. See our [verbal commitment guide](/guide/verbal-commitment-vs-nli/) for what this means in practice.

**June 6, 2025:** The court approved the House v. NCAA settlement, which lets Division I schools that take part pay athletes directly. See our [House settlement guide](/guide/house-settlement/) for a comprehensive breakdown.

**July 1, 2025:** Revenue sharing began with the 2025-26 academic year, allowing those schools to pay athletes directly from institutional revenue.

These are real, consequential changes. What they mean for your family depends on your athlete's sport and school — which is why the questions below matter more than any headline.

## Revenue sharing — what it actually is and who gets it

Under the House settlement, participating schools could share up to $20.5 million with student-athletes during the **2025-26** academic year — the settlement's first year, and the most recent figure in the NCAA's implementation Q&A. The cap is set at 22% of the averaged athletic revenue of the five defendant conferences plus Notre Dame, totaled across eight NCAA financial-reporting categories. It is recalculated from that formula every three years, and rises four percent in the second and third year of each three-year period.

**The NCAA's implementation Q&A (updated February 11, 2026) gives no 2026-27 dollar figure.** Treat $20.5 million as the 2025-26 number it is, not as the current one, and ask each program what its pool actually is.

The cap applies to what the NCAA calls Participating Institutions, a Division I term. Every Division I school in one of the five defendant conferences — the ACC, Big Ten, Big 12, Pac-12 and SEC — is one: it is bound by the settlement as a defendant and did not "opt in." Any other Division I school chooses, and may opt in for the next academic year at any point in the settlement's ten-year term; for 2026-27 the NCAA's current Q&A says the College Sports Commission would circulate the opt-in process by March 1, 2026.

**Here's what matters for soccer and volleyball families: the cap is a school-wide number, and nothing in the settlement divides it among sports.**

The NCAA's House implementation Q&A is explicit about what the cap is. It counts the school's own payments to athletes for the use of their NIL, up to $2.5 million of athletic aid above the old 2024-25 team limits, and up to $2.5 million of academic (Alston) awards; payments from third parties don't count (Question D7). It defines the benefits cap as "the maximum dollar value of revenue share payments or benefits a Participating Institution may provide to its student-athletes during an academic year," and it says each institution "may provide benefits, at its discretion, to a student-athlete" so long as the combined value across *all* of its student-athletes stays under that cap. That is a ceiling on the athletics department's total. It does not say what football gets, what basketball gets, or what soccer and volleyball get.

So the allocation is a decision each school makes on its own — school by school, year by year, and not published in any single place.

You may see percentage splits of the pool quoted in the press. The NCAA's Q&A sets none, and the court-approved Q&A for athletes says a school may pay "some, all or none" of its athletes — so we will not state a split here as though it were fact. What reaches a soccer or volleyball roster is each school's decision.

**Which is why the only number that matters is the one for that program.** Ask for it directly:

- Is this school a Participating Institution for the year my athlete would arrive?
- Does this program receive any revenue-share money at all?
- If it does, what was the total for this program last year, and how was it divided among players?
- Would any offer to my athlete be guaranteed beyond one year, and what conditions attach to it?
- Who decides the allocation, and at what point in the year is it decided?

The program's answers are the only figures that apply to your family.

## Third-party NIL — what's known for soccer and volleyball

**We have found no figures from the NCAA or another governing body for what soccer or volleyball athletes earn from NIL.** The figures that circulate — team totals, averages, medians by sport — come from news analyses and from companies that run NIL marketplaces, so we do not repeat them here as facts.

One thing is true by arithmetic, whatever the sport: a few very large deals pull an average far above what a typical athlete earns. If one athlete signs a $3 million deal and 99 others each earn $50, the average is just over $30,000, while the typical deal is $50. When you see an "average NIL" figure, ask for the median, for which sport, and from whose data.

For your athlete, the useful questions are concrete: what NIL activity have this program's current players actually had, and is any of it something your athlete could realistically expect? Treat a figure without a named source as a story, not a forecast.

**For an athlete on an F-1 student visa, NIL is an open legal question — and the NCAA treats it as off-limits on U.S. soil.** Here is who says what:

- **The NCAA's position is a flat prohibition.** Its *International Student-Athlete Handbook* states that international student-athletes with an F-1 visa "are prohibited from engaging in NIL deals while on U.S. land," and advises consulting the athletics compliance officer, the school's designated school official and/or an immigration lawyer before any NIL activity outside the country or any change to a P-1, O-1 or EB-1 status.
- **The federal rules do not address NIL.** F-1 regulations allow only specific categories of work, such as limited on-campus employment and authorized practical training (8 CFR 214.2(f)(9)–(10)), and "any unauthorized employment by a nonimmigrant constitutes a failure to maintain status" (8 CFR 214.1(e)). Neither those rules nor the federal definition of employment (8 CFR 274a.1) mentions name, image or likeness. In July 2021 the Student and Exchange Visitor Program said it was still assessing "whether regulatory guidance is required"; we have not found later guidance.
- **Revenue sharing is less settled still.** The NCAA's House settlement implementation guidance does not mention visas or F-1 status, though it describes a school's payments to athletes as payments for the use of their NIL — the activity the Handbook bars on U.S. soil. We have found no NCAA statement applying that prohibition to revenue-share payments, or saying it does not apply.

**For families with international athletes:** no agency has said that NIL or revenue-share income is available to an athlete on an F-1 visa. The people who can answer for a specific athlete are the school's compliance office and an immigration lawyer — not any website, including this one.

## The NIL Go portal and what it means for new recruits

The reporting rules matter for families to understand, even if your athlete's deals are small. Under the NCAA's House implementation Q&A (updated February 11, 2026):

- **Every Division I athlete must report each third-party NIL contract or payment worth $600 or more to NIL Go,** within five business days of agreeing to the payment terms. Several payments from the same payor that together reach $600 must be reported within five days of reaching that total. That applies at schools outside the settlement too.
- **A "third party" is anyone other than the school** or an entity the school owns, operates or controls.
- **NIL Go is a platform created by the College Sports Commission, with assistance from Deloitte,** to determine whether a deal with an associated entity or individual advances "a valid business purpose" and stays within "a reasonable range of compensation."
- **An athlete may submit a proposed deal for review before signing it.**

**For families during recruiting:** if a coach or a collective's representative promises specific NIL dollars, ask for the terms in writing and take them to the school's compliance office before relying on them.

If you encounter NIL promises during recruiting conversations, treat them as you would any unverifiable claim — note them, and check them before they shape a decision.

## What NIL and revenue sharing can tell you in recruiting

This is the practical section — what you can actually learn, and what you can't.

**What you can't learn from outside a program:** what a soccer or volleyball roster receives from the school's revenue-sharing pool, and what its players earn from NIL. Neither is in any NCAA document we have read, and neither shows on a roster, so a comparison of programs on these points rests on whatever each program tells you.

**What you can ask:**

- **For the revenue-share figure for your athlete's sport** at each program — the questions above.
- **About any NIL promise** made during recruiting, in writing, and run it past the compliance office.
- **Whether any figure is guaranteed** beyond the first year, and what conditions attach.

**The factors that have always shaped a college choice are still there to weigh:**

- **Athletic fit at the program** — position depth, class-year gaps, recruiting style (see [roster intelligence](/roster-intelligence/))
- **Academic fit and major availability** — test scores, acceptance rates, graduation rates
- **Coaching staff stability and program direction** — tenure, recruiting networks, playing style
- **Geographic preferences and family proximity**
- **Cost reality** — including how athletic scholarships, academic aid, and need-based aid stack together (see our [athletic scholarships guide](/guide/athletic-scholarships/))
- **Roster opportunities and realistic playing-time projections**

How much weight NIL and revenue sharing deserve beside them is your family's call, and it is easier to make once a program has given you its actual figures.

## Every recruiting journey is different

No two athletes have the same NIL experience, just as no two athletes have the same recruiting journey. Whether your athlete's sport receives any revenue-share money is each school's decision, and so is how it is divided among players. International athletes on F-1 visas face an open legal question about NIL, covered above.

NIL and revenue sharing are real parts of the modern college landscape. For soccer and volleyball recruits, the way to understand them is program by program, from the program's own answers — alongside the fit questions that have always mattered.
